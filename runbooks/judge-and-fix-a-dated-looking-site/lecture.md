# Judge and fix a dated-looking site, the lecture

Evidence for the steps in [RUNBOOK.md](RUNBOOK.md).

## 1. The lecture

1. **Course.** Department of Design and Communication, "Visual Persuasion for Small Services", week 6: why a page reads as old or as cared for. The question of the week: a stranger decides in a blink whether this business is current, and the answer is made of patterns, not of the business. Which patterns carry that signal now, and which carried it ten years ago?
2. **The idea that organises the week: the signal is a distance, and the reference point moves.**
   1. *Prototypicality* (Tuch and colleagues, 2012, Google Research): how representative a design looks for its kind of site. Users rated screenshots for beauty after as little as 17 to 50 ms: "both visual complexity and prototypicality play crucial roles in the process of forming an aesthetic judgment. It happens within incredibly short timeframes between 17 and 50 milliseconds". "If the visual complexity of a website is high, users perceive it as less beautiful, even if the design is familiar. And if the design is unfamiliar ... users judge it as uglier, even if it's simple".
   2. Synthesis, not in the source: if familiarity is part of appeal, then "looks dated" is the gap between a page and what is typical now. The study did not test age. The typical page changes over years, so a page frozen at 2015 slides away from it. No study I found tests an old look directly.
   3. *Aesthetics lend credibility.* Robins and Holmes (2008): "regardless of how low a credibility rating was given to a web site, some type of aesthetic treatment increased the rating for the same content in 19 out of 21 cases (90%)". Same content, different treatment, so the look moves trust before any word is read. This is lab work on mostly 2000s pages.
3. **What each era's own writers listed as the trend.** (Trade blogs with a stake in selling design; read as what was fashionable, not as what worked.)
   1. *2016, said to be rising:* the hamburger menu moving from phones to desktops, "a lot of websites use the Hamburger menu to keep the design clean and simple"; home page carousels, "definitely trending"; parallax scrolling, "unbeatable" for engagement (Trootech, although the same author warns about performance and SEO). Flat design "will stay for good". DeveloperDrive's 2016 list: parallax scrolling, "see-through" ghost buttons, grid design, the hero image "taking up the entirety of above the fold space", a gallery under it, and the hamburger menu.
   2. *2026, said to be rising:* bento grids ("has become one of the most recognizable design patterns of the mid-2020s", line25); type as the hero ("have replaced the stock hero image as the default attention-grabber", :71); variable fonts; motion that does not slow the page; dark mode chosen on purpose; accessibility as standard (tinyfrog).
   3. *2026, said to be fading* (tinyfrog): generic stock photography "increasingly reads as inauthentic", heavy parallax, auto-playing video, heavy carousels, and minimalism with no personality.
   4. Read together, three things the 2016 writers called trendy are now called dated by the 2026 writers: the carousel, parallax and the full-bleed stock hero. That is one blog's list against another's, not a measurement, but a design that matches a 2016 trend list is a design these writers now call dated.
4. **What the tested work says about those patterns.**
   1. *Hidden navigation.* Nielsen Norman Group, June 2016, a quantitative study: "Hidden navigation is less discoverable than visible or partially visible navigation", with a worse experience "in both mobile phones and desktop user interfaces", and on desktops "hiding navigation degrades the experience and the navigation discoverability more than it does on the phones". So the hamburger on desktop was a measured loss, not only a fashion.
   2. *Flat design with weak clickability cues.* NN/g (Moran, 2017): people spent "22% more time" and had "25% more fixations" on pages with weak signifiers. NN/g's own reading is that the cost was time spent finding where to click. Flat is not the fault; invisible buttons are.
   3. *Carousels and parallax.* The Trootech piece warned about performance in 2016 itself. NN/g's carousel video page says: "Sliding hero images that rotate through a set of promotions, news, or the like on the top of web pages are often annoying to users and are definitely error prone, unless they are designed according to usability guidelines". For parallax, the Purdue dissertation record (Frederick) reports that it "did not significantly improve overall user experience" but was rated "more fun" and "more professionally designed"; that is the dissertation, not the 2015 journal paper, and it is from a 2013 study.
   4. *Bootstrap.* The complaint is sameness, not the framework: a 2013 guide to making Bootstrap look unlike Bootstrap says "Bootstraps default font is Helvetica. Helvetica Helvetica Helvetica." and lists the font, buttons and navbar as the first things to change. A Bootstrap 3 look is dated in 2026 for the reason prototypicality predicts: it was the common template, and now it is the common template of a past year. No study measures how it reads.
   5. *The new generic.* The 2025 to 2026 version of the same sameness is the default AI-built page: a DEV post says Tailwind "popularized" indigo and violet by default and that AI reproduces the most common look (an opinion piece, quoted loosely). Expect the 2026 prototype to be dated by 2030.
5. **The classic mistakes students make.**
   1. Copying the trend list. A bento grid with nothing to say in the cells is the 2026 carousel.
   2. Mistaking a rebuild for care. Pages say "cared for" by detail: real photographs of the real work, a consistent spacing scale, a real type pair, states on every control, a fast first paint, readable contrast.
   3. Fixing only the first screen. Dated patterns live below it: footer clutter, a 2015 social icon row, a stock-photo "team", a form that asks too much.
   4. Treating flat, minimal or dark as the point. NN/g's study separates flat from invisible controls.
   5. Judging with your own taste. The reader is the persona, not the designer.
   6. Calling a pattern dated because a blog said so. The blogs of 2016 said the carousel was in.
