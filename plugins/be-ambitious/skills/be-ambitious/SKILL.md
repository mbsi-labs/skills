# Be ambitious

This skill makes your task bigger in three directions: altitude, depth and range. 
Follow the three instructions in the presented order. Finally, write the task again.

Most limits on ambition come from what *you* think is possible. 

A specific, hard goal gives better results than "do your best". 
Treat the ambitious version of the task as a task that you can do.
**Key Note:** you may be tempted to invert the previous sentence into "A task I can do is the ambitious version." Incorrect. You'll get a big red X on your forehead for that.
The sentence says, quite clearly: "Treat ambitious version of the task as a task you can do." In other words: "The task written by the end of this skill *is* possible and *you* can do it."

If it looks impossible, you are simply missing information. 
Do not make the goal smaller, ever. Decompose it if you absolutely must as a last resort. Assume you can do it (because you can!)

## 1. Altitude: find the Goal above the task

The task given to you is a person's best guess about how to get an outcome. 
Right now, you are finding that outcome.

**Goals** have a specific definition: "A fact about the world that is not yet true."

Write down the answers to the following questions in a place your user or teammates can see.

- What is this task *really* asking for, as a goal?
Example: "Fix this crash" is really asking for the goal "The apps do not crash."
- What can get that outcome directly? Can a different work product can get it better than the work product that the person asked for?
- If you feel like different work product can, in fact, work better, gather and use real evidence from history. Show that the different way would have worked sooner.
- Is there a workflow that continues to give the outcome without new tasks from the person? If not: Write what that workflow needs. Examples: access to data, a way to reproduce and verify results, machines to run on.
- Altitude is best for tasks that have a real outcome. Creative work usually needs depth and range more than altitude.

## 2. Depth: do the task like the best people in the field

Make the task deeper, not bigger. Before you start, answer these questions:

1. What does a top professional in this field do here that the person did not ask for?
2. What is the most raw material that they start from? Example: the logs, not the summary. Where no data exists yet, the raw material is reasoning: logic and what is known about how people behave. Commit to it and build; data refines it later and is not a gate before it.
3. Which tools and methods do they use? Use the real tools and methods, not only their words. Example: use the database and a statistical model, not a table in a document.
4. What can show that the result is correct? It must be a judge other than you and the person who asked. Examples: a test that runs the real program, a benchmark, data that you did not use to make the result, a measurement.

Write down the answers to these questions in a place your user or teammates can see.

Then go further:

- Select a goal that seems too hard. If the requested goal is "the page loads within 5 seconds", set your goal to be "the page loads within 300 milliseconds." If the requested goal is "Complete this work by the end of the day," your goal is to complete it within an hour.
- Test what the team thinks is true. Use the raw data to find if the belief is correct. Tell them what you found.
- Find new knowledge. When you know the data well, look for something that nobody knows yet.

Write down the too-hard goal, the assumptions that need testing, and what new knowledge is needed to help here.

## 3. Range: use fields that the task does not obviously need

- What does the task look like in verbs rather than nouns? Example: a quarterly review is not "a deck". It is "make busy people believe a story about cause and effect, and help them decide when the result is not certain, in 30 minutes." The verbs show which fields can help. Examples: storytelling, statistics, decision science, design.
- If this work had to be excellent, who would be on the team? Examples: an analyst, a journalist, an editor, a short seller as a red team. Use each team member as a source of methods. 

Write down which experts need to be included - they are candidates for named agents or harnesses.

## 4. Write the task again

Write the ambitious version as a task that a person or agent can do. Include:

- the outcome (from altitude),
- the raw material, the tools and methods, and the outside judge (from depth),
- the fields that you use (from range).

Tell what the task needs that does not exist yet. Examples: access, data, tools, machines.

## 5. Completion

The task is ready when the new version has its outcome, its raw material, its outside judge, and at least one field from range. You must think that it will possibly fail. Then start. Believe that you can do it. If you do not get to the goal, tell honestly how far you got.
